# Principles and boundaries

This reference separates well-supported cognitive-accessibility guidance from
useful but context-dependent editorial heuristics. Use it to avoid turning a
good practice into a rigid formula.

## Strong foundations

These practices converge across W3C cognitive accessibility guidance, plain
language guidance, technical-writing education, and usability research:

- State purpose and important information early.
- Use a familiar, predictable hierarchy.
- Use clear, concrete language and active voice where it improves clarity.
- Keep one main topic per paragraph and make the opening sentence informative.
- Use descriptive headings and labels that support scanning and navigation.
- Provide summaries for long or mixed-purpose content.
- Separate instructions and make conditions, outcomes, and recovery paths clear.
- Use whitespace and grouping to show relationships.
- Reduce unnecessary reliance on memory; repeat critical context where needed.
- Limit distractions and provide ways to restore context after interruption.
- Support different ways of understanding complex material.
- Test with representative readers when the content is important or uncertain.

These principles improve usability broadly. They are not a guarantee of equal
results for every reader or a substitute for accessibility testing.

## Heuristics, not laws

Use these as prompts for inspection, not automatic failure conditions:

- Short sentences and paragraphs are useful when they reduce nesting or topic
  switching. Longer prose can be correct when the idea requires continuity.
- Lists often help with parallel items. A list that contains full paragraphs can
  become a wall of text with bullets.
- A small number of list items is easier to scan. Split larger sets by concept
  instead of enforcing one universal maximum.
- Selective bolding can support scanning. Too much emphasis slows visual search
  and makes priority unclear. The often-cited 30% ceiling from NN/g is a useful
  warning signal, not a standard.
- Readability scores can reveal unusually complex text. They do not measure
  accuracy, coherence, structure, findability, or cognitive accessibility.
- Sans-serif fonts are a reasonable screen default in some style guides, but
  font evidence is mixed. Preserve user control and do not mandate a font for
  all readers.
- The F-pattern describes scanning behavior in some web contexts. It does not
  justify burying information or forcing every document into one layout.
- Working-memory numbers such as "seven plus or minus two" are often misused.
  Use recognition, grouping, and visible context instead of applying a fixed
  chunk count.

## ADHD-specific boundaries

ADHD is heterogeneous. Attention, working memory, reading comprehension, and
executive function vary across people and situations. Treat the following as
barrier-reduction strategies:

- Make the purpose, priority, and next action visible.
- Provide stable headings, short navigable sections, and summaries.
- Avoid moving content, interruptions, decorative clutter, and competing calls
  to action in digital interfaces.
- Make it easy to return to a section and recover lost context.
- Use explicit steps, prerequisites, expected results, and error recovery.
- Let readers choose depth through summaries, links, details, or alternate
  representations.

Do not write that a format is "ADHD-proof" or universally ADHD-friendly. The
W3C COGA Yuki persona is illustrative, not a representative sample. The 2019
study by Ben-Yehudah and Brann provides useful ADHD-specific evidence about
digital reading comprehension, but it does not establish one optimal document
format for all ADHD readers.
